From Playing Games to Designing Experiences

An Introduction to Game Aesthetics: Design and Implementation

Hi everyone, I’m Lao Li.

The introductory video for this series is now out. This article takes the discussion a little further: alongside a guide to the series, we’ll use seven game screenshots to make feelings such as “this looks beautiful” or “this feels satisfying to play” more specific.

You don’t need specialist vocabulary or an open game engine. Let’s start with one question: How do games create the experiences we feel while playing?

1. How Many Different Ways Can a Jump Feel?

Imagine a block standing on a platform. Press a button, and it jumps.

Changing its texture changes its appearance. Add a little squash before takeoff, a puff of dust on landing, and a short sound effect, and the jump may acquire a different sense of weight and force.

Now change the distance between platforms, the jump height, or the wait before restarting after a failure. Similar movements may start to feel relaxed or tense.

At least four things are working together: the image, the sound, the movement, and the rules governing what the player must do. Their relationships keep changing as the character moves, the player makes choices, and the situation develops.

View original

Figure 1 — Celeste: find the character, possible footholds, and hazards. Source: official store launch trailer, 00:26.

Start with something concrete: where is the character, where could they stand, and what should they avoid? The red-haired character, pale spikes, and platform edges establish different shape and color relationships against the darker architecture.

A still image helps us inspect these details. To understand movement speed, the transitions between jumping, dashing and landing, or the time before another attempt, we need continuous footage and actual play. A screenshot alone cannot explain why the controls feel good.

Our study of game aesthetics brings sensory presentation, action, and experiences produced by rules into the same discussion. Narrative, personal experience, and cultural context will also enter where relevant.

2. From “Atmospheric” to “What Can I Actually Observe?”

Beautiful, oppressive, light, weighty: these words are useful starting points because they describe our responses. The next step is to ask which visible or playable details may contribute to them.

Hollow Knight: Silhouettes and Layers

View original

Figure 2 — Hollow Knight: inspect the character’s silhouette and the relationship between dark structures and the bright background. Source: official store release trailer, 00:14.

The small white figure is surrounded by large dark structures and a bright background. Follow the outlines: where does the character separate from the environment? Which structures sit in front, and which forms recede?

“This scene feels ethereal to me” describes a response. “Dark structures surround a bright area, with the character suspended below the center” records an observation. Connecting the two gives us something to discuss.

Whether everyone experiences this composition that way, and how much sound contributes, requires further observation. We can offer an explanation while leaving room for alternatives.

Journey: Scale Through the Character and Environment

View original

Figure 3 — Journey: notice the figures’ size, the diagonal dunes, and the open space. Source: official store tenth-anniversary trailer, 00:40.

Start with relative size. The figures occupy little of the frame, while sand and sky fill most of it. The slopes also lead our eyes across the image.

That suggests a question: if the camera moved closer so a character occupied more of the frame, would our sense of distance, direction, or scale change? This could become the starting point for an experiment.

To discuss the emotions of the journey more fully, we would need to bring movement, music, sound, and surrounding events back into the analysis. Screenshots let us pause and inspect; continuous experience helps us understand change.

INSIDE: Placing a Character Within an Environment

View original

Figure 4 — INSIDE: compare the red-shirted figure, the crowd, and the light above them. Source: official store mini-trailer, 00:04.

Describe the difference between the red-shirted figure and the other silhouettes, then examine the light above and the surrounding darkness. Compared with the dunes in Journey, what kind of space encloses these people? Where does your attention go?

If you feel uneasy, record that response. Then return to continuous footage to look for evidence: how do people move, where do they pause, and when do environmental sounds appear? An adjective becomes a question we can investigate.

These observations are entry points. Returning to the same games with questions about movement, sound, rules, or narrative will gradually deepen our understanding.

3. Three Abilities We’ll Practice

The first is aesthetic appreciation: describing specific details and testing interpretations through comparison. How does watching a movement as a still differ from watching it unfold? Does muting sound change your perception of impact timing or force? The result might be an observation record supported by details and a clearly identified clip.

The second is aesthetic design: starting from a desired player experience and deciding how visuals, sound, movement, space, and feedback should work together. If a character should feel light, write that goal down before proposing movement rhythms and sounds. A one-page style guide can explain the reasons behind those choices.

The third is practical implementation: turning a proposal into a playable comparison, inviting others to try it, and checking our expectations. Assets, time, and technical constraints shape our choices. Which adjustment best supports the intended experience within the resources available?

We’ll repeatedly follow this process: observe a specific moment → propose an explanation → design a comparison → implement and playtest → revise from the results.

For example, to explore a heavier landing, change only its sound while keeping the jump trajectory, camera, and level unchanged. Compare the results, then study deformation or dust separately. Once we understand their individual contributions, we can investigate combinations.

Even when a playtest matches our expectations, we should record the conditions. A different scene or group of players may produce different responses.

4. How the Six Modules Fit Together

Six modules organize the knowledge we’ll build. Videos will move between them according to the question being explored.

  1. Aesthetic theory: shared vocabulary, description, comparison, research questions, and evaluating experiments.
  2. 2D aesthetic design: silhouettes, composition, color, lighting, animation, sound, and feedback.
  3. 2D aesthetic techniques in Godot: image display, graphics and shader fundamentals, 2D lighting, animation, and sound experiments.
  4. 3D aesthetic design: form, materials, space, cameras, and atmosphere.
  5. 3D aesthetic techniques in Godot: materials, lighting, stylized rendering, and spatial audio.
  6. Aesthetic prototypes and tutorials: small playable projects documenting goals, changed variables, observations, and revisions.

For a jumping topic, we might first ask why a jump feels tense, consider movement and sound choices, build a Godot experiment, and return with playtest notes. Relevant theory can be introduced as the need arises.

Pixel art, hand-drawn imagery, silhouettes, low-poly forms, and cartoon styles will enter through specific questions. What information does a chosen form preserve, and how does it work with sound, movement, and interaction?

5. Recurring Examples and Guest Games

Celeste, Hollow Knight, Journey, and INSIDE are planned as recurring examples for the first round of the series. Other games will join when a topic benefits from their particular approach.

Trace the black outlines to identify the character and terrain, then examine the structures in the gray background. With little color information, edges, value differences, and spatial relationships deserve close attention. What further information would sound and movement supply during play?

These games will enter the series through relevant topics. Godot experiments will use original or clearly sourced simple assets to explore observable principles, with the differences between our teaching implementations and the original games made explicit.

6. Choose a Learning Path Around Your Questions

If you’re primarily a player, start with the examples and comparisons in Game Aesthetics Discussions, then watch experiment demonstrations and playtest reviews as your interests lead you. Programming is not required for these observations.

If you’re learning design or making an independent game, focus on experience goals, reasons for design choices, and production tradeoffs. Bring those questions back to your own work.

If you want to build things in Godot, take a question from the discussions into Godot Aesthetics Lab, complete an experiment, and use the results to reassess the design. Technical installments assume basic programming and Godot skills, such as creating scenes, attaching scripts, and running a simple project. Graphics and shaders will be introduced progressively from the fundamentals, with their purpose explained along the way.

If you’re moving from playing games toward making them, begin with observation and design proposals, then build your programming and engine foundations.

Each video will explain its question, demonstration, and takeaway. The accompanying Patreon articles will gradually develop definitions, evidence, design reasoning, and experiment records in a form suited to closer reading. Start with the question that matters to you now.

7. Make Your First Observation Card

Choose a short game moment you like, preferably containing one clear action: a jump, an attack, a brief exploration, or a choice. Record the following:

My Game Aesthetics Observation Card

Game and version: ________

Location: level, scene, or video timestamp: ________

Action taking place: ________

My strongest feeling: ________

Two or three details I directly observed: ________

My hypothesis about how those details contribute: ________

One condition I would change for comparison: ________

For a still-image example, we can use Figure 3:

  • Location: Journey’s official tenth-anniversary trailer, the dune scene at 00:40.
  • Feeling: The space feels expansive to me.
  • Observations: The figures occupy little of the frame; sand and sky fill most of it; the slope runs diagonally across the image.
  • Hypothesis: The relative size of the figures and environment may contribute to that sense of openness.
  • Comparison: In an original test scene, would moving only the camera closer change that feeling?

This example practices observation of a still image. With a playable action, add movement and sound details. Keep “what I saw or heard” separate from “why I think it works.”

Your completion checklist is simple: one identifiable moment, one main feeling, two or three concrete details, and one question you can explore through comparison. Keep the hypothesis open until you can test it.

Share your observation card and screenshot here on Patreon. You can also bring a moment from your own project that still feels slightly off. We’ll work from these specific questions toward clearer descriptions and playable ideas.

The next round begins with a discussion of jumping, followed by a Godot jumping experiment with sound and a playtest review. Specific clips and parameters will be documented as those installments develop.
